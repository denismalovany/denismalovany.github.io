<!-- Context -->
<div class="mb-12">
  <h2 class="text-xl font-bold mb-4 section-title section-title--mb-6">Context</h2>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    A volunteer workshop was repairing specialized optical equipment. One problem kept coming back: the most expensive and scarce component was burning out at an alarming rate. The root cause was not a manufacturing defect &mdash; it was user behavior. Operators were turning on the equipment in daylight or bright conditions, instantly destroying the component. The manual existed, but nobody read it.
  </p>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    Each burned-out component meant the device was stuck on a shelf &mdash; not because the repair was difficult, but because replacements were scarce and logistics were slow. The problem wasn't technical. It was communication.
  </p>
</div>

<!-- Approach -->
<div class="mb-12">
  <h2 class="text-xl font-bold mb-4 section-title section-title--mb-6">Approach</h2>
  <div class="space-y-6">

    <div>
      <h3 class="text-base font-semibold mb-2">1. Workshop &mdash; Separating Human Errors from Inevitable Failures</h3>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
        I brought together engineers and department leads from across the workshop. Each team shared their most frequent pain points on sticky notes. We evaluated every issue on two parameters &mdash; repair speed and part cost &mdash; and sorted them into categories. Some failures were objective: wear and tear, manufacturing tolerances, field conditions. Those went to the engineering teams. Others were set aside &mdash; problems that would resolve themselves as logistics improved, or that were simply beyond what our project could influence.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mt-2">
        One problem stood apart: the most expensive component kept burning out because <strong>operators skipped the manual and turned on the device in daylight</strong>. This was a <strong>human-caused failure</strong> &mdash; preventable through communication, not engineering. That's why I took ownership of it.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div>
      <h3 class="text-base font-semibold mb-2">2. From Insight to Multi-Channel Response</h3>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
        The SMM team proposed an educational campaign. What started as "let's post a tutorial on social media" quickly grew into a coordinated multi-channel effort. I collected the handling rules from engineers &mdash; the critical do's and don'ts that would prevent burnout &mdash; and distributed them across two tracks:
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mt-2">
        <strong>Social media track</strong> &mdash; the SMM team independently produced a video series and supporting materials for our channels, reaching the wider community.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
        <strong>Physical track</strong> &mdash; something placed directly with the equipment, reaching operators at the exact moment they first held the device. This became the brochure.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div>
      <h3 class="text-base font-semibold mb-2">3. Brief &amp; Wireframe &mdash; Translating Rules into a Story</h3>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
        Technical rules like "do not activate in bright light" are easy to ignore. I structured the information not as a list of prohibitions, but as a short narrative sequence &mdash; what the operator needs to know, in the order they need to know it. The wireframe defined the flow, the key messages for each panel, and how the folding structure would reveal the story step by step.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div>
      <h3 class="text-base font-semibold mb-2">4. Handoff to Graphic Designer</h3>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
        I handed the brief and wireframe to the graphic designer. During our discussion, the comic format emerged naturally &mdash; an illustrated dialogue between the device and the operator. The device "speaks" to explain how to handle it, while the operator asks the questions a real user would have. This made the rules feel like a conversation, not a lecture.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div>
      <h3 class="text-base font-semibold mb-2">5. Delivery &mdash; Into Every Box</h3>
      <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
        The finished brochure &mdash; a compact z-fold &mdash; was placed inside every repaired device returned to operators. The physical format mattered: it couldn't be ignored or scrolled past. It was right there when the operator opened the box, before the first power-on.
      </p>
    </div>

  </div>
</div>

<!-- Result -->
<div class="mb-12">
  <h2 class="text-xl font-bold mb-4 section-title section-title--mb-6">Result</h2>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    Repeat component burnouts decreased after the brochure was introduced. While multiple factors may have contributed, two signals stood out: fewer devices returned with the same damage pattern, and the brochure itself was noticed.
  </p>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
    Operators gave unsolicited positive feedback &mdash; they appreciated the format, understood the rules, and in some cases mentioned specific panels that had changed how they handled the equipment. The comic approach had done what the original manual never could: it got read.
  </p>
</div>

<!-- Key Takeaways -->
<div class="mb-12">
  <h2 class="text-xl font-bold mb-4 section-title section-title--mb-6">Key Takeaways</h2>
  <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-3 text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">
    <li><strong>Workshops reveal what you don't know you know.</strong> The engineers had all the information. The workshop simply gave it a structure &mdash; and a matrix that made priorities impossible to ignore.</li>
    <li><strong>Match the format to the moment.</strong> A manual on a website competes with everything else. A brochure in the box meets the operator at the exact moment of first use &mdash; no friction, no search, no scroll.</li>
    <li><strong>Rules remembered are better than rules listed.</strong> Turning prohibitions into a story &mdash; a dialogue, not a lecture &mdash; made the information stick.</li>
    <li><strong>Coordinate, don't just produce.</strong> My job wasn't to design every pixel. It was to extract the right information from engineers, structure it, and hand it to the right people &mdash; SMM for video, graphic designer for the brochure &mdash; in a form each could run with.</li>
  </ul>
</div>
