Case Study

Breather Coach — Respiratory Training App

Timeline

2021–2022

My Role

UX/UI Designer — sole designer on the project at the time, inheriting a project with significant design debt

Team

Me (designer) + development team (rotated multiple times before and during the project)

Product Site

thebreather.com ↗

Context

The Breather is the world's first respiratory muscle training (RMT) device — a drug-free, clinically proven tool by PN Medical that helps people strengthen their breathing muscles in just 5 minutes a day. Used by over 2 million people, it serves everyone from seniors with respiratory conditions to elite athletes and military pilots.

The Breather Coach app was designed to be the device's digital companion. Its core job: teach users how to use the device correctly, track their training sessions, and let them log how they feel — essentially digitizing what users had been doing in notebooks. For the company, the app was also a critical tool for FDA clinical trials, where structured data collection and protocol compliance were non-negotiable.

But when I joined the project, it was in serious trouble. The app had accumulated significant design debt — the interface was a patchwork of inconsistent components, the color palette had accumulated countless variations (for example, many barely-distinguishable shades of blue), and the underlying tech stack was outdated.

Worse, the app had a very low App Store rating. Reading through user reviews, I found two distinct problem categories: technical issues (crashes, glitches, instability) and UX issues — users simply didn't understand what they were supposed to do inside the app or why they needed it at all. My first job wasn't to design something new — it was to understand what was broken and bring order to the chaos.

Approach

1. App Store Review Analysis

Before touching a single screen in Figma, I started where users were already talking: the App Store reviews. The app had a very low rating, and reading through the feedback revealed a clear split. Most users complained about technical stability — the app crashed, froze, and glitched. But a significant portion of reviews pointed to a deeper UX problem: users didn't understand what they were supposed to do inside the app, how it connected to the device, or why they needed it at all. This gave me two clear problem dimensions to investigate.

2. Client Briefing & Stakeholder Alignment

I conducted an in-depth briefing with the client to understand their vision, expectations, and business goals. Through this conversation, I learned about their competitors, how they positioned themselves in the respiratory training market, and what they wanted the app to ultimately achieve. The client's stated goal was to improve the App Store rating — which meant aligning stakeholder priorities with user evidence became a key part of my work.

3. Design Audit & Inventory

My first hands-on task was to map the existing state. I catalogued every screen, component variant, and color value across the entire app. This audit quantified the fragmentation: duplicate components with subtle inconsistencies, color values that differed by imperceptible amounts (for example, a proliferation of barely-distinguishable shades of blue), and interaction patterns that shifted unpredictably between screens. The inventory became the definitive map of the implementation gap — the distance between what existed and what should exist.

4. Design System Unification in Figma

With the audit complete, I consolidated all UI elements into a single, coherent design system. This meant: reducing the color palette to a defined set, standardizing components so the same element looked and behaved identically everywhere, and establishing consistent spacing, typography, and interaction rules. The goal was that any screen in the app should feel like part of the same product — regardless of which prior designer originally touched it.

5. Automation with Figma Plugins

To accelerate the unification process, I used Figma plugins to partially automate repetitive tasks — batch-renaming layers, swapping color styles, and detecting inconsistencies across large numbers of screens. This turned what could have been weeks of manual cleanup into a faster, more systematic process.

6. FDA Clinical Trial Module Design

The project's priority then shifted to the FDA-facing clinical trial module — the closed portion of the app used in studies to validate the device's medical efficacy. I designed structured training screens that followed precise medical protocols, and patient questionnaires for logging physical state and wellbeing throughout the day or after each training session. The FDA module had to balance rigorous data collection with a patient experience clear enough for participants of varying ages and health conditions.

7. FDA Documentation & Submission Support

I prepared the testing and usage documentation required for the FDA submission package — including step-by-step instructions for clinical staff administering the trials and visual walkthroughs of the app's data collection screens. This documentation bridged the gap between design intent and regulatory requirements, ensuring the clinical team could deploy the module without ambiguity.

8. Behavioral Analytics from Backend Data

Toward the end of my time on the project, I worked with the backend developers to extract user behavior data. What we found was striking: while the number of registered users was growing, the number of users who actually completed at least one training session was falling. Even the users who pushed through the difficult registration process were dropping off at their very first training — a clear signal that the interface, not just the sign-up, was failing. This confirmed what the App Store reviews had hinted at months earlier.

9. Usability Testing with Real Users

In parallel with the analytics work, I conducted a small usability study. I created a typical user scenario — a common task flow through the app — and broke it into discrete steps. For each step, I tracked whether users could complete it independently, needed a hint, or found it completely non-obvious. The result was sobering: nobody could complete the full scenario without help. The app's complexity was systemic — users didn't struggle with individual screens; they struggled with the entire mental model. One specific finding stood out: the registration flow was a major point of friction. The app demanded too much personal information upfront — far more than users who had just installed it were willing to share.

10. Proposed UX Solutions

With both qualitative and quantitative evidence in hand, I designed a set of targeted improvements as my final contribution to the project. First, an onboarding flow for first-time users — a guided introduction that taught people how to use the app before asking anything of them. Second, a fundamental restructuring of the registration timing: instead of demanding personal information upfront, the app would let users complete their first training session immediately, then prompt registration afterward. The argument: their training history would always be saved, so there was real value in signing up — and having already invested the effort of a first session, users were far more likely to take that final step.

I also redesigned the registration flow itself — breaking it into clear, sequential steps instead of a single overwhelming form, making each field's purpose obvious, and eliminating any personal information that wasn't strictly necessary at this stage. Unfortunately, these improvements were not implemented. The development team's rebuild took priority, and my time on the project concluded. The designs remained as a validated, research-backed blueprint for the next version.

Result

The design system was fully unified at the Figma level — all components, colors, and patterns standardized into a single source of truth. While the development team didn't have the bandwidth to implement every change at the code level, the Figma design system became the definitive reference for any future work on the app, saving the next team from starting from the same fragmented state I inherited.

The FDA clinical trial module was successfully built and deployed in real patient studies — the design moved from Figma to actual clinical environments where it collected structured data on training adherence, patient wellbeing, and device efficacy. The trials spanned both medical treatment applications and military pilot training scenarios. The documentation I prepared became part of the FDA submission package.

Toward the end of my time on the project, the usability study and behavioral analytics revealed the full picture of what was broken in the user journey — from a burdensome registration to a first-training experience that caused even motivated users to drop off. The data validated the diagnosis I had suspected since reading the App Store reviews.

The proposed UX solutions — onboarding, delayed registration, simplified sign-up — weren't implemented before my time concluded. But they remain a validated, research-backed blueprint grounded in both qualitative observation and quantitative funnel data, ready for when the rebuild team is ready to address the user experience.

Key Takeaways

  • Evidence beats opinion when aligning stakeholders. The usability study — showing that no user could complete the full scenario unaided — was far more persuasive than any design argument could have been. Data shifted the client's focus from fonts to fundamentals.
  • On messy projects, start with the fundamentals. Consistency before innovation. A scattered design system creates friction for every subsequent feature — fixing it is a force multiplier for everyone who touches the product next.
  • Design systems outlive implementation timelines. Even when developers can't rebuild to match, a well-organized Figma file is a durable asset that accelerates the next attempt.
  • Healthcare UX demands dual fluency. Designing for FDA trials means speaking two languages — user-centered design for patients AND regulatory rigor for compliance. Screens must be both usable and auditable.
  • Automation amplifies design leverage. Figma plugins turned a manual cleanup slog into a systematic process — a small upfront investment that saved weeks.
  • Research-backed solutions have value even when unshipped. The onboarding flow, restructured registration, and simplified sign-up weren't implemented — but they remain a validated blueprint grounded in real user behavior data, ready for the next rebuild.
  • Problem-solving on troubled projects is a skill in itself. Inheriting a project with significant design debt means the challenge isn't just design — it's navigating technical debt, misaligned priorities, and fragmented context. Resilience and pragmatism matter as much as craft.
