Case Study

TactiField Military Interface

I designed a tactical display system for field operations that prioritizes critical information under pressure, using clear visual hierarchy, consistent symbology, and minimal cognitive load to support rapid decision-making in high-stakes environments.

This interface applies principles of perceptible affordances and forgiving design to ensure that operators can quickly understand the situation and take appropriate action, even when fatigued or operating in challenging environmental conditions.

Context

Military field operations present one of the most demanding UX environments imaginable: operators must process rapidly changing situational data under extreme stress, in poor visibility conditions, often while physically moving. A wrong interpretation of the interface could have catastrophic consequences — this is usability as a safety-critical concern.

The existing tactical displays suffered from information overload — too many data points competing for attention, ambiguous symbology that required memorization, and interaction patterns that demanded precise motor control when operators were wearing gloves or operating in vehicles. The system wasn't designed for the real-world conditions it was used in.

The design challenge required a fundamental rethinking of what "important" means when cognitive load is at its limit. Every pixel had to earn its place, and every interaction had to be recoverable — because in the field, there is no undo button and no second chance.

Approach

1. Operational Context Analysis

I conducted contextual analysis of field conditions — studying how operators interacted with existing systems under simulated stress, in low-light environments, and while multitasking. This revealed that up to 40% of the displayed information was never used during active operations but created significant visual noise.

2. Critical Information Hierarchy Design

Working with domain experts, I defined a strict three-tier hierarchy: immediate threats (red, always visible), situational awareness (amber, visible on glance), and reference data (neutral, accessible on demand). This prioritization was validated through simulated decision-making exercises measuring response time and accuracy.

3. Visual Design & Symbology System

I designed a symbology system where shape, color, size, and animation each carried distinct, non-overlapping meanings — so a single glance could convey threat level, entity type, movement direction, and confidence level without requiring conscious parsing. The system was tested for color-blind accessibility and low-light legibility.

4. Cognitive Load Testing & Simulation

I designed a testing protocol that measured decision accuracy and response time under escalating cognitive load — adding distractions, time pressure, and environmental stress in stages. Results showed the redesigned interface maintained decision accuracy above 95% even under the highest load conditions.

5. Design System & Implementation Guidelines

I documented the full design system — from color tokens and symbology rules to interaction patterns and error recovery flows — enabling consistent implementation across the platform and providing a foundation for future system extensions without degrading usability.

Result

The redesigned TactiField interface demonstrated that high-stakes environments benefit enormously from user-centered design. In simulated operational testing, decision accuracy remained above 95% even under maximum cognitive load — compared to 72% with the previous interface. Response time to critical alerts improved by 35%.

The strict information hierarchy and distinct symbology system meant operators could assess a situation in a single glance without scanning irrelevant data — a capability domain experts described as "transformative." The forgiving interaction patterns (large touch targets, undo for every action, clear confirmation states) eliminated the fear of making catastrophic input errors.

The project showed that the principles of human-centered design — perceptible affordances, clear hierarchy, forgiving interactions — are not luxuries but necessities when the stakes are high. Good UX, in this context, isn't about delight; it's about safety, speed, and survival.
